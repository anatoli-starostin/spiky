Lookup tables → spiking networks · Walker2d

A lookup table that learned to walk — and then became a spiking network

We wanted to show that lookup-table models transfer to the spiking domain in practice, not just in principle. So we trained one to control a Walker2d robot, turned it into a pure spiking neural network by direct construction, and measured whether the robot still walked. It does — indistinguishably from the software policy it came from.

2,889 neurons · 25,953 synapses · ~155 ticks per decision · no external clock · no gradient descent inside the network · running live in the browser demo.

1The idea in one picture

Spiking neural networks are attractive because they are cheap in hardware: a neuron does nothing until a spike arrives, and information can live in when a spike happens rather than in a multiplied number. The usual difficulty is getting a good network into that form. Training directly in spikes is hard; converting a trained conventional network is lossy.

A lookup-table (LUT) policy sidesteps both. It never multiplies anything. It only ever compares its inputs to each other, uses the pattern of comparisons as an address, and reads out a stored value. Comparisons are the one thing spike timing does natively — whichever neuron fires first wins — so the translation is a construction, not an approximation. Almost every weight and delay in the finished network is computed in closed form from the trained table.

2Part 1 — training a lookup table to walk

The architecture: compare, address, look up

The policy holds 32 independent lookup tables. Each table owns 6 fixed pairs of observation coordinates — its anchor pairs. For an observation x, each pair contributes one bit: 

bit_i   = 1[ x[a_i] > x[b_i] ]        6 anchor pairs per table, packed MSB-first
addr_t  = the 6 bits of table t       →  one of 64 rows
row_t   = weights[t, addr_t]          weights: (32 tables, 64 rows, 6 outputs)

mu[o]   = 32·τ · log( (1/32) · Σ_t exp( row_t[o] / τ ) )      τ = 0.09377
action  = clip(mu, −1, 1)

That is 12,288 learnable numbers (32 × 64 × 6) plus a single learned temperature τ, and — this is the load-bearing part — no arithmetic is ever performed on the observation. The policy only asks which of two coordinates is larger. The addressing is exact rather than approximate, and "which is larger" is precisely the question a race between two spikes answers.

Training it: PPO on 201 million environment steps

The table was trained from scratch with a GPU-resident PPO implementation (PyTorch + NVIDIA Warp for the batched physics — not JAX), running 8,192 Walker2d environments in parallel.

Four deliberate constraints — and why each one exists

A freely trained policy is continuous at both ends: it reads real-valued observations and emits real-valued torques. A spiking network can do neither. So after the main run we did a short 384-update quantisation-aware fine-tune that put the physical limitations into the training loop, where the policy could adapt to them instead of suffering them at conversion time. Each constraint answers a specific property of the spiking substrate.

Constraint 1  Input: 128 Gaussian-companded buckets, one shared map

A spike happens at an integer tick, so the observation must become an integer before it can enter the network. We map each coordinate through tick = round(127 · Φ(x/σ)) — a Gaussian companding curve that spends its resolution where observations actually live, rather than uniformly across a range they rarely reach.

The map is shared across all 17 coordinates out of necessity, not convenience. The LUT addresses by comparing coordinates to each other; a per-coordinate map would silently change the meaning of every address bit. The width σ = 1 was measured, not assumed: it is an interior minimum of the address-bit flip rate (0.486%, against 1.39% at σ = 0.5 and 1.16% at σ = 3.0), and it coincides with the principled value for unit-variance observations.

Constraint 2  Output: the soft (log-sum-exp) pooling

This one was not added for quantisation — it is the architecture. But it is the reason the readout is buildable at all. As Part 2 shows, a synapse can supply an exponential, a dendrite can supply a sum, and an exponentially growing membrane supplies a logarithm as its time-to-threshold. A log-sum-exp is therefore something a single neuron computes physically. A plain sum would have been fine too; a softmax or a matmul would not.

Constraint 3  Output: clip to [−1, 1], then 22 uniform levels

The spiking readout reports its answer as the tick at which a neuron fires — an integer. The action is therefore inherently discrete, at a step of 2/21 ≈ 0.0952. Rather than discover this after conversion, we trained through it, using a straight-through estimator so gradients still flow. The policy learns to be good at the actions it can actually express.

Constraint 4  An L2 penalty on out-of-band outputs — the one that mattered most

The first fine-tuning run raised return by +411 and yet made the spiking network worse. The reason: the policy's raw, pre-clip output sprawled far outside [−1, 1] — about 51% of components — and the fine-tune left it exactly as sprawled while making the table weights wider.

That is structural, not bad luck. The clip's gradient is exactly zero outside the band, and an out-of-band action is free twice over: MuJoCo clamps ctrl to its range (verified — +3.3 and +1.0 produce bit-identical trajectories), and the training environment clamps before computing control cost. Nothing anywhere pulled the raw value back in. But in the spiking network the raw value is a physical span — of delays, then of weights — and sprawl costs ticks. So we made it cost something in training too: 

loss += w · mean_batch( Σ_o relu( |mu_raw[o]| − 1 )² )        w = 0.3
The penalty weight was chosen by a 20-update sweep, not guessed. Pulling the raw output in shrinks the delay span the spiking network has to physically realise.

An honest caveat about the word "quantised"

The table weights themselves were never quantised. They remain float32 — 12,286 distinct values out of 12,288, on no grid whatsoever. "Quantisation-aware" here means at the interfaces: what enters the network and what leaves it are discrete; what is stored inside is not. (Part 3 later puts the readout weights on an 8-bit grid, but that is a separate step with a different motivation.)

The result of Part 1 is an ordinary, competitive Walker2d policy with a natural gait, which happens to be expressible as 12,288 stored numbers and a pile of comparisons.

Stage 1  Order detection — races, not subtractions

Every observation coordinate is emitted as a first-spike latency: bigger value, earlier spike. 

tick = 127 − searchsorted(bucket_edges, x_norm)      larger value → EARLIER spike

Only the bucket edges cross into the network. It consumes tick ordering and never the dequantised value, so the software-side "what number did that bucket mean" table never has to exist in hardware. Ordering is preserved exactly, ties included — which is why Stage-1 agreement with the software LUT is bit-exact: 100.0000%, 0 wrong bits out of 98,304, not merely close.

The comparator: an unstable equilibrium that remembers

136 dual-rail winner-take-all pairs resolve the 192 address bits (many bits share an anchor pair). Each pair is two neurons that excite themselves and inhibit each other. The crucial detail is that the rails are anti-leaky — their membrane grows away from rest rather than decaying towards it. That makes V = 0 an unstable equilibrium: the neuron that gets its input first runs away upward and fires; the other runs away downward and stays silent for the rest of the episode.

This matters because the 17 input spikes are spread over roughly 85 ticks. A decision made at tick 12 has to survive until tick 127. A leaky comparator would forget it. An anti-leaky one self-reinforces the decision and holds it with no maintenance current at all.

Membrane traces on the real kernel showed exactly why ties were a problem: the cross-inhibition lands one tick after the excitation it must cancel. On an exact tie both rails see a clean +1.519 and both fire. One tick apart, the loser sees +1.5 − 10 = −8.5 in a single tick and never rises. Adding one tick of delay to the greater-than rail (DE_GT = 4) aligns the tie case with its veto, leaves the one-apart case untouched, and reproduces the software's strict > convention exactly.

Self-timing: the network has no clock

A single leak-free completion detector takes all 17 inputs at weight 0.06 with threshold 1.0. Since each input fires exactly once, 16 × 0.06 < 1.0 ≤ 17 × 0.06 makes it fire precisely when the last input arrives — no earlier, no later. That one neuron replaces what used to be an external fixed-tick stimulus, so the network now takes no external drive at all: it is triggered entirely by its own inputs and finishes when it is finished.

It then waits out the comparator pipeline (D_GATE = 6), because the last rail resolves at t_last+3 and its tie veto at t_last+5. Gating any earlier was measured, and cost real accuracy — 99.9196% bit parity with 78 cases where two cells fired at once.

Stage 2  Lookup — one-hot by coincidence, with a proof

2,048 neurons, one per lookup cell. Each listens to the six memory cells that spell out its address. Six coincident spikes at weight 0.18 give 1.08 > 1.0 and it fires; five give 0.90 < 1.0 and it does not.

The cells are leak-free, and that is what turns a heuristic into a guarantee. A wrong cell at Hamming distance k from the correct address receives exactly 6 − k spikes, so its membrane rises monotonically to at most 0.90 and stays there for all time — there is no transient that could overshoot the threshold and no decay that could lose a correct one. Measured over 16,384 table selections: 0 tables selected nothing, 0 tables selected more than one.

Stage 3  The readout — where the physics does the arithmetic

This is the part that makes the whole thing work, and it is worth stating plainly: the output neuron does not compute a log-sum-exp; it is one.

Each selected cell connects to each output with delay 1 and weight β_o · exp(w_t / τ) — the stored table value lives in the amplitude of the synapse. All 32 selected cells therefore land on a single tick, and the membrane integrates V₀ = Σ_t exp(w_t / τ) in one step. The output neuron is anti-leaky and exactly linear, so from there V(t) = V₀ · e^(t/τ_eff), and its threshold crossing is 

T = τ_eff · ( log θ − log Σ_t e^(w_t/τ) )
  = const − (τ_eff / 32τ) · mu

The synapses supply the exp. The dendrite supplies the sum. Time-to-threshold under exponential growth supplies the log. That is the entire pooling operation from Part 1, evaluated by physics rather than by arithmetic.

An earlier version of the design encoded each stored value as a delay instead. It is algebraically equivalent — because the output neuron is exactly linear, shifting arrival times by a_t = A − (τ_eff/τ)·w_t gives the same result up to the constant β_o = α_o · e^(−A/τ_eff) — but it is much more expensive:

Amplitude encoding is not merely faster; it is also strictly more faithful. The delay path had to round every delay to an integer tick, so it evaluated a quantised log-sum-exp — worth up to a quarter tick of error. It also depended on synaptic integration across ticks, which this kernel does not provide.

Self-timing has a consequence for the decode

Because the network starts when its inputs arrive rather than on a clock, the origin of time moves with every observation. The decode therefore must be referenced to the completion event — T − t_last, not T. Using an absolute reference was tried, and within-one-level accuracy collapsed from 100% to 50–70%.

The lattice — an elegant choice that came with a bill

TAU_M_OUT was set to 31.257 so that 22 ticks span exactly 22 output levels. Neat — but it makes the decode lattice pitch equal the output grid step: 0.09523729 against 0.09523810. Three consequences follow, and the third one cost us a lot of time.

  • Every decoded value sits at the same phase relative to the output grid. So shifting the decode offset moves no samples across a level boundary, or all of them. We verified this the hard way: an on-policy offset recalibration left five of six action dimensions bit-identical, flipped the sixth wholesale, and scored −175 in the walker.
  • The closed-form model can be checked. Solving it against the network's measured spike ticks gives phase 0.750 and base +13 — the same values on all six dimensions, independently. That agreement is the evidence the model is structurally right.
  • The rounding bias is one-sided. The crossing tick is the ceiling of a continuous time, and the decode slope is negative. So rounding the tick up always rounds the action down. Not sometimes — always.

4Part 3 — the one thing we had to fit

At this point the network was a faithful construction and still −164.8 ± 50.3 of return behind its teacher. The lattice argument above says the bias cannot be removed by recalibrating the decode — any offset shift moves nothing or everything. Adding output levels does not help either; 22 levels can already represent the right answers. But changing individual stored weights is not degenerate with a global offset, and that is the opening.

Coordinate descent, no gradients

Two gradient-based attempts failed first, and both failures were informative: a straight-through optimiser diverged outright (the loss rose 29%), and the closed-form model was not faithful enough to optimise against until it was phase-pinned to the network's measured ticks. Coordinate descent avoids both problems, and the structure of the network makes it very cheap:

weight (t, k, o) affects only output dimension o, and only those states whose table t happened to select cell k — and it enters through a sum. So evaluating a candidate move is just S′ = S − exp(w_old) + exp(w_new) over the affected states alone.

  • Objective: level-mismatch against the software teacher's 22-level action, scored by the phase-pinned closed form with the tick ceil inside the loss — so the optimiser sees the same rounding the hardware will.
  • Constraint: weights confined to an 8-bit log-domain grid, enforced during the fit rather than applied afterwards. (8 bits was shown to cost nothing versus full precision, and sits comfortably inside the sensitivity bound: a relative weight error δ shifts the crossing by τ_eff·δ, so half an output level needs δ < 1.6%.)
  • Data: 153,600 teacher input→output pairs from 512 independent rollouts, split by seed — 115,200 train (seeds 0–2), 38,400 held out (seed 3).
  • Two mandatory gates before any move was accepted: the scorer had to reproduce the true network's baseline behaviour, and a zero-step run had to reproduce that baseline exactly.

Loss fell from 151,004 to 17,100 in three sweeps — about six seconds. 5,390 of the 12,288 weights moved, with a maximum log-domain drift of 0.261. Validated on the real spiking network, on GPU, with measured spike ticks, on held-out states:

The one-sided −1 mass collapses from 15–26% to 1–2% on every dimension, with a matching +1 mass — symmetric, roughly 15× smaller, and not an aggregate cancellation of two large opposite errors.

The weights and the decode offset are one object

Changing the weights changes the crossing ticks, so the fit also re-derives the decode offset — by −0.22 to −0.32 of a level. We learned this the expensive way: an evaluation that combined the fitted weights with the old offset mis-decoded by a quarter of a level (the same magnitude as the bias just removed) and scored −197. That number supported a confident and completely wrong conclusion — that return had somehow decoupled from action agreement.

With its matching offset, the very same set of weights scores +58.8. The two are a pair and must never be used apart; the evaluation harness now refuses to run weights without an offset, and both are baked together into the shipped artefact.

So the honest summary of "how much training happened inside the spiking network" is: none, in the usual sense. No backpropagation, no surrogate gradients, no spike-time learning rule. A gradient-free search adjusted 5,390 numbers on a discrete grid to compensate for a known, characterised rounding artefact of the readout.

6It is live

The network is deployed in the interactive Walker2d stand, selectable as “Spiking LUT quantised (handcrafted SNN)” alongside the software policies. It drives a real MuJoCo simulation on the server, one independent instance per viewer, and both spike-visualisation panels — the raster and the network graph — populate from its actual activity.

The actor is pure NumPy at inference: no PyTorch, no SciPy. It runs on CPU at roughly 6 ms per action against a 33 ms frame budget at 30 steps per second.

7What we are not claiming

+58.8 is not a win

The spiking network scored higher than its teacher. At 1.31σ that is noise. The correct reading is "indistinguishable", and the number should not be quoted as an improvement. There is no mechanism by which discarding information would make the policy better.

Other limits worth stating plainly:

  • Table weights are not quantised. Only the interfaces and the readout weights are on grids. A genuinely all-integer model is further work.
  • The fit used teacher-visited states only. All 153,600 pairs come from the software policy's own trajectories. It transferred to closed-loop control at 98.58% agreement — but there was no guarantee in advance that it would.
  • One task, one policy. This is an existence proof on Walker2d, not a general conversion pipeline with error bars across a benchmark suite.
  • The tick budget is a real cost. ~155 ticks per decision is cheap for this simulator, but it is a latency floor that a matmul does not have.

Four traps, each of which produced a wrong answer before it was caught

  1. Censored evaluation. Any rollout shorter than the 1,000-step episode limit measures only the episodes that failed — the successful ones never complete and are never counted. A 600-step "cheap screen" returned n = 1 and a meaningless mean.
  2. Exact-match is the wrong calibration objective. Maximising exact agreement re-selects the biased-low decode. The objective that matters is a zero mean signed residual.
  3. Underpowered evaluations. At n = 65 the standard error is ~110, so a real effect of ~165 sits inside the noise. ~520 episodes brings it to ~30–40.
  4. Weights and offset are a pair. Covered above — it cost the single most confidently wrong conclusion in this whole line of work.
