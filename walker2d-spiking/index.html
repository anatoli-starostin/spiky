Lookup tables → spiking networks · Walker2d

A lookup table that learned to walk — and then became a spiking network

We wanted to show that lookup-table models transfer to the spiking domain in practice, not just in principle. So we trained one to control a Walker2d robot, turned it into a pure spiking neural network by direct construction, and measured whether the robot still walked. It does — indistinguishably from the software policy it came from.

1The idea in one picture

Spiking neural networks are attractive because they are cheap in hardware: a neuron does nothing until a spike arrives, and information can live in when a spike happens rather than in a multiplied number. The usual difficulty is getting a good network into that form. Training directly in spikes is hard; converting a trained conventional network is lossy.

A lookup-table (LUT) policy sidesteps both. It never multiplies anything. It only ever compares its inputs to each other, uses the pattern of comparisons as an address, and reads out a stored value. Comparisons are the one thing spike timing does natively — whichever neuron fires first wins — so the translation is a construction, not an approximation. Almost every weight and delay in the finished network is computed in closed form from the trained table.

The order matters. The policy is trained first, as ordinary software, with a handful of constraints chosen so that it is later buildable in spikes; the spiking network is constructed from the finished table afterwards. Part 1 is the training, Part 2 the construction.

2Part 1 — training a lookup table to walk

The task: 17 numbers in, 6 torques out

Walker2d is a standard MuJoCo control benchmark: a two-legged figure in the vertical plane with six hinge joints — thigh, leg and foot on each side. The policy is called once per control step and sees 17 numbers; it emits 6.

The 17 are torso height, torso angle and the six joint angles (8 positions), then the torso's forward, vertical and angular velocity and the six joint angular velocities (9 velocities; clipped to ±10 before the policy sees them). Forward position is deliberately left out, so the policy cannot tell how far it has already walked. The 6 are one torque per joint, each in [−1, 1].

Reward is forward velocity, plus a bonus for each step spent upright, minus a small control cost. The episode ends when the torso leaves a healthy height band (0.8 – 2.0) or tilts past 1 radian — that is, when the walker falls. Walking fast without falling is the whole task.

The architecture: compare, address, look up

The lookup-table idea is not ours. It was introduced in the Spiking Manifesto (E. Izhikevich), which argued for tables addressed by spike-order comparisons as the natural computational primitive for a spiking substrate. What follows is that idea implemented and optimised in spiky: a differentiable, GPU-resident LUT layer — fast_multi_head_lut.py — trained end to end by policy gradient, then compiled into an actual network of spiking neurons.

The policy holds 32 independent lookup tables. Each table owns 6 fixed pairs of observation coordinates — its anchor pairs. For an observation x, each pair contributes one bit: 

bit_i   = 1[ x[a_i] > x[b_i] ]        6 anchor pairs per table, packed MSB-first
addr_t  = the 6 bits of table t       →  one of 64 rows
row_t   = weights[t, addr_t]          weights: (32 tables, 64 rows, 6 outputs)

mu[o]   = 32·τ · log( (1/32) · Σ_t exp( row_t[o] / τ ) )      τ = 0.09377
action  = clip(mu, −1, 1)

That is 12,288 learnable numbers (32 × 64 × 6) plus a single learned temperature τ, and no arithmetic is ever performed on the observation. The policy only asks which of two coordinates is larger. The addressing is exact rather than approximate, and "which is larger" is what a race between two spikes answers.

Training it: PPO on 201 million environment steps

The table was trained from scratch with a GPU-resident PPO (proximal policy optimisation) implementation (ppo.py — PyTorch + NVIDIA Warp for the batched physics, not JAX), running 8,192 Walker2d environments in parallel. 82,953 parameters, 201M environment steps.

Four deliberate constraints — and why each one exists

A freely trained policy is continuous at both ends: it reads real-valued observations and emits real-valued torques. A spiking network can do neither. So after the main run we did a short 384-update quantisation-aware fine-tune (ppo_qat_obs.py) that put the physical limitations into the training loop, so the policy could adapt to them rather than meet them for the first time at conversion. Each constraint answers a specific property of the spiking substrate.

What "quantised" means here, in the software teacher

During PPO, the teacher's table weights are never quantised. They stay float32 — 12,286 distinct values out of 12,288, on no grid whatsoever. "Quantisation-aware" at this stage means at the interfaces: what enters the policy and what leaves it are discrete; what is stored inside is not.

That is a statement about the software teacher only. The spiking model built from it in Part 2 is a different object, and its readout weights are genuinely quantised — to 8 bits, in the log domain. That is what the word means in the deployed model's name, and it is the subject of Part 3.

Constraint 1  Input: 128 Gaussian-companded buckets, one shared map

A spike happens at an integer tick, so the observation must become an integer before it can enter the network. We map each coordinate through tick = round(127 · Φ(x/σ)) (obs_quant.py) — a Gaussian companding curve that spends its resolution where observations actually live, rather than uniformly across a range they rarely reach.

The map is shared across all 17 coordinates out of necessity, not convenience. The LUT addresses by comparing coordinates to each other; a per-coordinate map would silently change the meaning of every address bit. We used σ = 1, which turned out to be close enough to the real observation distributions.

Constraint 2  Output: the soft (log-sum-exp) pooling

This one is architectural, and the reason is Dale's law: every neuron single-signed, with all of its outgoing synapses excitatory or all inhibitory, never a mixture. Real neurons are like that. You either design for it from the start or fail it retroactively.

The log-sum-exp is what makes it possible in the output stage. A Stage-2 neuron delivers β_o · exp(w/τ) to an output neuron, and an exponential is always positive, so every readout synapse is excitatory whatever the sign of the stored table value. The signs and the ordering are not lost — the logarithm on the other side recovers them, and that logarithm is not arithmetic we perform but the time-to-threshold of an anti-leaky output neuron. The exponential is in the synapse, the sum in the dendrite, the logarithm in the membrane.

A plain sum computes the right thing — we measured that it trains and quantises just as well. But building one out of spiking neurons would need genuinely signed synapses: inhibitory ones, on neurons that are excitatory elsewhere, which breaks the single-signed rule by construction. That, not the numerics, is why the exponential is here.

Dale's law holds for the whole network, not just the readout

By construction, every presynaptic neuron's outgoing weights are single-signed: 2,610 purely excitatory, 272 purely inhibitory, zero mixed. The only negative weight anywhere is a single value emitted by the 272 winner-take-all rail interneurons of Stage 1. One small inhibitory population doing one job; everything else excitatory.

It is also biologically motivated — motivated, not accurate. A perfectly non-leaky integrator is an idealisation; anti-leaky membrane dynamics are not. The persistent sodium current INaP and dendritic calcium plateau potentials both produce membranes that accelerate rather than decay, and the exponential integrate-and-fire model carries an exp() term for the sodium upstroke. The anti-leaky output neuron has a hook in that literature; it is not an arbitrary convenience.

Constraint 3  Output: clip to [−1, 1], then 22 uniform levels

The spiking readout reports its answer as the tick at which a neuron fires — an integer. The action is therefore inherently discrete, at a step of 2/21 ≈ 0.0952. Rather than discover this after conversion, we trained through it (act_quant.py), using a straight-through estimator so gradients still flow. The policy learns to be good at the actions it can actually express.

Constraint 4  An L2 penalty on out-of-band outputs

This constraint exists for one asymmetry: sprawl is free for the task and expensive for the spikes. Nothing in the reinforcement-learning problem discourages the policy from emitting a torque of +3.3 where +1.0 would do. The clip's gradient is exactly zero outside the band, MuJoCo clamps ctrl to its range (verified — +3.3 and +1.0 produce bit-identical trajectories), and the training environment clamps before computing control cost. An out-of-band action is free twice over, so the table can sprawl as far as accuracy wants.

The spiking network pays for it anyway, because everything the table asks for has to be realised — as neurons, as synapses, and as dynamic range the readout must resolve inside a finite tick budget. So we made sprawl cost something in training too: 

loss += w · mean_batch( Σ_o relu( |mu_raw[o]| − 1 )² )        w = 0.3
The penalty weight was chosen by a 20-update sweep, not guessed. The first fine-tuning run — no penalty — raised return by +411 and still made the spiking network worse, because it left the readout exactly as sprawled while widening the table weights.

Out-of-band output is reduced, not removed. At the lighter weight we later used for ablations (0.1), 15–18% of the raw readout is still outside the band after a full run. The clip still has zero gradient out there — the penalty simply adds the only live gradient that pulls inward, and it is fighting an objective that does not care.

Part 1 ends with an ordinary, competitive Walker2d policy with a natural gait — one that happens to be expressible as lookup tables: 12,288 stored numbers and a pile of comparisons.

3Part 2 — building the spiking network

Everything from here on is construction. The three stages map one-to-one onto the three things the LUT does: work out the comparisons, use them as an address, and pool the results. The whole build is one script — tiny_lut_quantised_pipeline.py, which takes no arguments — and it verifies itself against the software table as it goes.

First, the substrate: what spnet actually simulates

The network runs on spnet (spnet.py), our spiking simulator — originally a precise CUDA implementation of Izhikevich's 2006 spnet, later extended to support different neuron types. It is worth a short detour: several things that look like design decisions later are properties of this substrate.

Time is discrete. The simulation advances in integer ticks. A spike is an event at a tick boundary — there is no sub-tick timing, and nothing ever happens between ticks. A synapse is just two numbers, a weight and an integer delay in ticks (1 to 255): a spike emitted at tick t lands on its target at tick t + delay.

A neuron is a handful of coefficients. Every neuron's membrane follows the same quadratic (Izhikevich) ODE, and a neuron type is nothing but a choice of its coefficients, a threshold, and a reset rule: 

v′ = cf₂·v² + cf₁·v + cf₀ − u + I         spnet.py:14

    leaky integrate-and-fire :  cf₂ = 0,  cf₁ = −1/τ     spnet.py:57
    our anti-leak rails      :  cf₂ = 0,  cf₁ = +1/τ

That is the whole of the "one sign flip" you will meet in Stage 1 — the anti-leak neurons are not a special mechanism, they are the ordinary machinery with cf₁ given the opposite sign. When v reaches the threshold the neuron emits a spike and resets, and the input current I is delivered per tick rather than accumulated across ticks.

The ODE is integrated by Euler. Each tick is stepped as two Euler half-steps of dt = 0.5 (N_EULER_STEPS 2, EULER_DT 0.5f in the kernel). That is a detail, but two of its consequences run through the rest of this page:

  • Because ticks are integers, a threshold crossing can only be reported as a whole tick — the true crossing time is continuous, but what the network can say is the ceiling of it. That is the seed of the decode lattice, and of the one-sided rounding bias that Part 3 has to remove.
  • Two half-steps of Euler are not the same as the exact exponential, so the membrane's effective growth constant is slightly larger than its nominal τ: τ = 31.257 gives τ_eff = 31.506. Everything downstream uses τ_eff, which is why that second number keeps appearing.

So: integer ticks, weight-and-delay synapses, and a membrane that is three coefficients and a threshold. Nothing else. Every mechanism in the three stages below is built out of exactly those pieces.

Stage 1  Order detection — the comparator, in full

This is the core of the network, so here is one comparison unit synapse by synapse. Everything below is the wiring as it is actually built, in tiny_lut_quantised_pipeline.py and, identically, in the deployed actor.

Every observation coordinate is emitted as a first-spike latency: bigger value, earlier spike. 

tick = 127 − searchsorted(bucket_edges, x_norm)      larger value → EARLIER spike

Only the bucket edges cross into the network. It consumes tick ordering and never the dequantised value, so the software-side "what number did that bucket mean" table never has to exist in hardware.

Self-timing: the network has no clock

One neuron watches for the end of the input window: a single leak-free completion detector taking all 17 inputs at weight 0.06 with threshold 1.0. Since each input fires exactly once, 16 × 0.06 < 1.0 ≤ 17 × 0.06 makes it fire precisely when the last input arrives — no earlier, no later. The spike it emits is the gate pulse. That one neuron is what removes the need for an external fixed-tick stimulus, so the network takes no external drive at all: it is triggered entirely by its own inputs and finishes when it is finished.

The problem: a decision made at tick 12 has to survive until tick 127

The 17 input spikes do not arrive together. They are spread across roughly 85 ticks, because that is what latency coding means — a large coordinate fires almost immediately, a small one fires much later. A comparator whose two inputs both happen to be large might settle its answer at tick 12; another might not settle until tick 120.

But the lookup in Stage 2 needs all 192 address bits at once. So every early decision has to be held, unchanged and unmaintained, until the last one is in. A conventional leaky neuron cannot do that: whatever charge encodes the decision decays away long before the window closes.

Six neurons, ten synapses, no loops

One comparison unit — deciding whether coordinate A spiked before coordinate B — is exactly six neurons: two rails r0/r1, two inhibitory interneurons i0/i1, and two memory neurons m0/m1. There are 136 such units, covering the 192 address bits (bits that compare the same pair of coordinates share a unit).

The complete synapse list for one unit. There are no recurrent synapses anywhere in this network — no neuron connects to itself, and neither rail connects to the other. The inhibition is feed-forward-crossed: each interneuron is driven by an input, not by the rival rail, and drives only the opposite rail. Ten synapses × 136 units, plus the detector's 17 inputs and Stages 2–3, reproduces the shipped total of 25,953 synapses exactly.

The one sign flip that makes it work

A normal leaky neuron obeys v′ = −v/τ. Its voltage always slides back to zero: zero is a valley, and any decision written into the membrane rolls back down into it.

The rails invert that single sign. They are not leaky-integrate-and-fire neurons at all but a raw membrane with v′ = +v/τ, τ = 20 — in the engine's parameters, cf_1 = +0.05 where a leaky neuron would carry −0.05. Now zero is a hilltop: an unstable equilibrium. A membrane sitting exactly at zero stays there, but the instant it is pushed it accelerates away and never comes back.

What that buys

The rail that receives its +1.5 first runs away upward, crosses threshold and fires. The rail that receives the −10 veto first runs away downward — and keeps going, for the rest of the episode. Measured on the real kernel, a vetoed rail reads −8.61, −9.04, −9.50, −9.98, −10.49 …, multiplying by 1.0506 every single tick. By the time the gate pulse arrives ~120 ticks later it is near −3,400. No later excitation can rescue it; +1.5 against −3,400 is nothing.

So the decision is held by the losing rail's own membrane sign. Not by a feedback synapse, not by a maintenance current — there is nothing to maintain. That is how a decision made at tick 12 is still intact at tick 127.

The memory neurons are an AND gate

m0 is a leaky neuron with threshold 1.0 and exactly two inputs, each worth +0.6: one from its rail, one from the completion detector. Neither can fire it alone (0.6 < 1.0); together they give 1.2 and it fires. It is a coincidence detector.

So the rail spikes at some early, unpredictable tick and leaves 0.6 of sub-threshold charge behind. That charge leaks away with τ = 1200 — a time constant chosen precisely so that the worst case survives the whole input window: an early rail and a late gate are ~127 ticks apart, and 

0.6 · e^(−127/1200) + 0.6  =  0.539 + 0.6  =  1.139  >  1.0    ✓

Their purpose is re-timing, not memory. They convert "my rail fired at some unpredictable early tick" into "a spike at one common tick", which is what lets Stage 2 do a plain 6-way coincidence (6 × 0.18 = 1.08 > 1.0) without knowing anything about arrival order. The decision itself was already safe — held by the losing rail's runaway membrane.

D_GATE = 6 is the conduction delay on the two synapses that carry the gate pulse from the completion detector into a unit's memory neurons, and its job is to make the gate late enough. The detector fires at t_last+1, but the comparator is not finished then: the greater-than rail's excitation from that last input carries the extra tick of skew and lands at t_last+4, reaching its memory neuron at t_last+5. A delay of 6 puts the gate pulse at t_last+7, after every comparator has decided. Gating earlier was measured and cost real accuracy — at delay 1, 99.9196% bit parity with 78 cases where two lookup neurons fired at once.

Ties, and the one tick that fixed them

Everything above assumed a clear winner. Ties are not an edge case: there are zero exact float ties in the data, but once the inputs are bucketed, 0.95% of address slots and 77% of samples contain two coordinates in the same bucket.

Membrane traces on the real kernel showed why they broke the comparator: the cross-inhibition lands one tick after the excitation it must cancel. On an exact tie both rails see a clean +1.5187 and both fire before either veto arrives — two bits asserted where there should be one. (Both then get vetoed and dive away: −10.13, −10.64, −11.18, −11.74, −12.34 … — but too late, the spikes are already out.) One tick apart, the loser sees +1.5 − 10 = −8.5 in a single tick and never rises at all.

Ties are resolved by synapse #1's delay, and nothing else. That is exactly why the GT rail's excitation carries a delay of 4 while everything else in the unit is faster: the extra tick slides it from arriving one tick before the veto to arriving with it. On a tie the GT rail is therefore cancelled and stays silent, while the LT rail still fires — which is bit 0, exactly the software's strict >. The clear-winner case is unaffected, because there the veto was never simultaneous to begin with.

Ties are handled by the same six neurons and ten synapses that decide everything else. The gap between "fires" (+1.519) and "silent" (−8.606) is 10.1, against a threshold of 1.0.

The extra tick always lands on the right rail by construction, because our lookup tables deal only in ordered pairs (i, j) with i < j. That convention is what the comparators are built on: r0 is always the rail driven by the lower-indexed coordinate, so it is always the “greater-than” rail, and the skew is always on it.

Stage 2  Lookup — one-hot by coincidence

2,048 neurons, one per lookup cell. Each listens to the six memory neurons that spell out its address. Six coincident spikes at weight 0.18 give 1.08 > 1.0 and it fires; five give 0.90 < 1.0 and it does not.

The wiring is one rule. Each comparison unit has two memory neurons, so a lookup neuron never needs an inhibitory input: it takes its excitation from whichever of the two carries the value its own address requires.

Every comparison unit fires exactly one of its two memory neurons, so each bit delivers its 0.18 to precisely those lookup neurons that ask for the value it has just decided. Only the neuron whose address agrees on all six collects the full six.

The neurons are leak-free, and that is what turns a heuristic into a guarantee. A wrong neuron at Hamming distance k from the correct address receives exactly 6 − k spikes, so its membrane rises monotonically to at most 0.90 and stays there for all time — there is no transient that could overshoot the threshold and no decay that could lose a correct one. Measured over 16,384 table selections: 0 tables selected nothing, 0 tables selected more than one.

Stage 3  The readout — where the physics does the arithmetic

The output neuron does not compute a log-sum-exp; it is one.

Each selected neuron connects to each output with delay 1 and weight β_o · exp(w_t / τ) — the stored table value lives in the amplitude of the synapse. All 32 selected neurons therefore land on a single tick, and the membrane integrates V₀ = Σ_t exp(w_t / τ) in one step. The output neuron is anti-leaky and exactly linear — its membrane time constant is TAU_M_OUT = 31.257 ticks, which sets how fast it climbs, and τ_eff = 31.506 is the exact per-tick growth constant that follows from it once the engine's two Euler half-steps are inverted. So from there V(t) = V₀ · e^(t/τ_eff), and its threshold crossing is 

T = τ_eff · ( log θ − log Σ_t e^(w_t/τ) )
  = const − (τ_eff / 32τ) · mu

The synapses supply the exp. The dendrite supplies the sum. Time-to-threshold under exponential growth supplies the log. That is the entire pooling operation from Part 1, evaluated by physics rather than by arithmetic.

An earlier version of the design encoded each stored value as a delay instead. It is algebraically equivalent — because the output neuron is exactly linear, shifting arrival times by a_t = A − (τ_eff/τ)·w_t gives the same result up to the constant β_o = α_o · e^(−A/τ_eff) — but it is much more expensive:

Amplitude encoding is not merely faster; it is also strictly more faithful. The delay path had to round every delay to an integer tick, so it evaluated a quantised log-sum-exp — worth up to a quarter tick of error. It also depended on synaptic integration across ticks, which this kernel does not provide.

Because the network starts when its inputs arrive rather than on a clock, the origin of time moves with every observation, so the decode is referenced to the completion event: T − t_last. That reference is available two ways. It is the completion detector's own firing tick less its fixed one-tick latency — the detector fires at t_last+1, so its time-to-first-spike carries t_last. It is also just the largest of the 17 input ticks, which the encoder has already computed on the way in; that is the form the deployed decode uses, because it is free.

The output neuron's membrane time constant, TAU_M_OUT, is the one number in Stage 3 that was chosen rather than derived from the trained table. It controls how fast that anti-leak membrane climbs, and therefore how much output value a single tick of crossing time is worth — the pitch of the decode lattice.

At 31.257 ticks that pitch is 0.09523729 of output per tick, against an output grid step of 2/21 = 0.09523810 — equal to eight parts in ten million. So 22 ticks map onto 22 output levels, one tick per level: every tick the neuron could fire on lands on a value the policy can actually emit, and no resolution is wasted.

4Part 3 — the one thing we had to fit

spnet advances in whole ticks, so the readout can only fire on a tick boundary and every action it reports is quantised. That leaves a small residual inaccuracy, and no simple recalibration removes it. The workaround is a short gradient-free fit of the readout weights — and the same pass leaves those weights on an 8-bit grid, which is worth having on its own.

A short, gradient-free nudge to the readout weights

The structure makes the search almost free. Weight (t, k, o) affects only output dimension o, and only those states whose table t selected cell k — and it enters through a sum, so a candidate move costs just S′ = S − exp(w_old) + exp(w_new) over the affected states. Plain coordinate descent (stage3_cd_bigdata.py) against 153,600 teacher input→output pairs (115,200 train, 38,400 held out, split by seed), with the tick ceil inside the objective so the search sees the same rounding the hardware will.

All 12,288 Stage-3 readout weights (32 tables × 64 rows × 6 outputs) stay on an 8-bit log-domain grid throughout — 256 levels in w/τ, a step of 0.029. The constraint is enforced during the search rather than applied afterwards, and it costs nothing: a relative weight error δ shifts the crossing by τ_eff·δ, so half an output level needs δ < 1.6%, which 8 bits clears comfortably. Three sweeps, about six seconds, 5,390 weights moved, maximum log-domain drift 0.261. Validated on the real spiking network, on GPU, with measured spike ticks, on held-out states:

What spnet actually stores in the synapse

The grid and the synapse are not the same numbers. A spnet synapse is only ever a (weight, integer delay) pair, and the engine does no exponentiation at runtime — when a spike arrives it adds the stored number straight onto the postsynaptic membrane. So what sits in the synapse table is not the teacher weight w; it is the amplitude

A = β · exp(w/τ)        precomputed offline, before deploy

and w itself never appears in the running network at all.

The 8-bit grid sits one exp() step upstream of that, on w/τ — 256 levels anchored at lo = −5.099703 with a step of 0.029012245 — so the shipped synapse is A = β·exp(quantised w/τ). The artefact was checked against that grid and sits on it exactly, to float32 storage precision. The consequence is that the values in the synapse table are geometrically spaced, even though the grid they came from is uniform: the same 12,288 numbers, seen from the other side of the exponential.

Either way, quantising w/τ quantises the synapse — this is not a compression applied to a saved file, it is the weight the hardware would hold. That is what the deployed actor's name, "Spiking LUT quantised", refers to, on top of the quantised input and output interfaces it inherits from the teacher.

The one-sided −1 mass collapses from 15–26% to 1–2% on every dimension, with a matching +1 mass — symmetric, roughly 15× smaller, and not an aggregate cancellation of two large opposite errors.

The weights and the decode offset are one object

Changing the weights changes the crossing ticks, so the fit also re-derives the decode offset — by −0.22 to −0.32 of a level. The two are a pair and must never be used apart. Fitted weights read with the old offset mis-decode by about a quarter of a level — the same magnitude as the bias just removed — and they do it silently: no error, no warning, just a worse walker. Both are baked together into the shipped artefact, and the evaluation harness refuses to run weights without a matching offset.

So "how much training happened inside the spiking network" has a clean answer: none. No backpropagation, no surrogate gradients, no spike-time learning rule. A gradient-free search moved 5,390 numbers on a discrete grid to cancel a known, characterised rounding artefact of the readout — a few seconds of post-processing on top of a network that was otherwise built, not trained.

6It is live

The network is deployed in the interactive Walker2d stand, selectable as “Spiking LUT quantised (handcrafted SNN)” alongside the software policies. It drives a real MuJoCo simulation on the server, one independent instance per viewer, and both spike-visualisation panels — the raster and the network graph — populate from its actual activity.

Nothing is emulated. NumPy does the two ends — the companding that turns an observation into input ticks, and the decode that turns a firing tick back into an action — but everything between them is the network itself, built through spiky.spnet at construction and advanced tick by tick with process_ticks on every call (spiking_lut_quantised.py). What the stand runs is the real simulator, not a re-implementation of it. It still fits in roughly 6 ms per action on one CPU core, against a 33 ms frame budget at 30 steps per second.
